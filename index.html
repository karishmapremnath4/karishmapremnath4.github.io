KiCad · LTspice · ESP32-S2

5-Channel Mains Relay Controller — Schematic, PCB Layout & Circuit Simulation

Requirements → schematic capture → LTspice analysis → PCB layout → design review → fabrication package

A two-layer board that switches five mains appliances, either over WiFi or from the wall switch already on the wall. I designed it in KiCad, sized the relay driver in LTspice, and took it through to a released fabrication package. The part I value most is not the layout — it is the pin-map review that caught a dead channel before the board was ever ordered.

40Parts selected & routed
5Switched mains channels
0DRC errors at release
5.78 VClamped switch-off spike
7.8×Margin on the BC547 45 V rating
1Reserved-pin fault caught pre-fab

Wall switch · WiFi→ESP32-S2→470 Ω base→BC547→Relay coil + 1N4007→Mains load

Circuit design & analysis

  • Five identical driver channels: GPIO into a 470 Ω base resistor, BC547 low-side switch, relay coil clamped by a 1N4007. Inputs use a 470 Ω series resistor with a 10 kΩ pulldown so an open switch reads a defined low instead of floating.
  • Simulated the coil turn-off transient in LTspice both with and without the flyback diode, as a two-netlist pair differing by one line. Clamped, the collector peaks at 5.78 V against the BC547’s 45 V rating.
  • Unclamped, the simulation runs away because the SPICE model has no avalanche breakdown. The real transistor would avalanche at 45 V and absorb the coil energy itself on every release, which is the actual failure mechanism the diode prevents.
  • Local switch control runs independently of the network stack, so a channel still toggles from its wall switch when WiFi is down.

Layout, isolation & release

  • 230 V mains kept off the logic three ways: a transformer-isolated HLK-PM01 supply, five milled slots through the board in the high-voltage region to raise creepage, and separate track widths by domain — 2.0 mm for mains, 1.0 mm for coil and logic.
  • Trace capacity on the mains runs is roughly 4–6 A, below the relay contact rating, so the connected load has to be sized to the copper rather than to the relay. That constraint is written into the firmware README rather than left implicit.
  • All through-hole, both copper layers used, zero vias — every connection is inspectable by eye.
  • Released a full fabrication package: gerbers, drill files across seven tool sizes, and a BOM, with DRC clearing at zero errors.

Design review Channel 5 was originally assigned to IO26. On the WROVER module inside the SAOLA-1R, IO26 is SPICS1, hardwired to the PSRAM chip select, so it cannot serve as a GPIO while PSRAM is enabled. The board would have assembled, passed continuity and powered up with four working channels and one dead one, with nothing visible on the board to explain it. I caught it reviewing the schematic against the module pin map, moved the channel to IO38, and confirmed the fix reached the routed copper and the exported gerbers rather than stopping at the schematic. The pre-fix schematic is kept in the repository so the change is reviewable.

Scope note The schematic, layout, simulation and fabrication package are complete. The board has not been fabricated or assembled, and the firmware has not been compiled against hardware, so the 5.78 V figure is a simulation result and not a bench measurement.

Design & instrumentation

  • 33 field instruments specified with 4–20 mA loop design, calibrated measurement ranges, and a deliberate fail-safe failure direction chosen per loop.
  • ISA-5.1 P&ID plus a written control narrative covering operating modes, startup, shutdown and abnormal conditions.
  • 4 regulatory control loops, including one cascade pair and one ratio pair for chemical dosing.
  • 8-state batch sequence with permissives derived from measured process conditions rather than sequence state alone.

Implementation & verification

  • Ladder Diagram for motor control and safety interlocks; Structured Text for analog conditioning, PID and sequencing, per IEC 61131-3.
  • ISA-18.2 alarm rationalization: 12 alarms with assigned priority, setpoint, deadband and operator response.
  • 13 FAT cases + 26 logic tests. 6 logic defects found, rectified and retested to a 100% pass rate; every interlock tripped and its response recorded.
  • Controller exercised end to end against a first-principles plant simulator over Modbus TCP.

Scope note The control system, design documentation and test package are complete engineering deliverables. The process itself is a first-principles plant simulator driven over Modbus TCP, not physical equipment.

ML-Assisted PLC Structured Text Bug Detection & Rule-Based Repair

The defects that compile cleanly and fail in operation

A compiler will not tell you that an interlock has been bypassed or that a setpoint is wrong — the code is valid, the plant is not. This project targets exactly that class of fault: functional defects in PLC Structured Text that pass compilation and only reveal themselves during operation.

Dataset & features

  • Built a corpus of 300 IEC 61131-3 Structured Text programs seeded with 8 classes of functional defect — disabled interlock, incorrect setpoint and similar — each of which compiles successfully but fails during operation.
  • Python workflow performing automated feature extraction directly from source, turning control logic into a structured feature representation.

Classification & repair

  • Trained a decision tree classifier to predict the defect class, raising accuracy from an 81.7% baseline to 100% across 60 unseen programs.
  • Rule-based repair stage corrected all 60 single-defect test programs, each fix re-verified against expected behaviour.
  • Decision tree chosen deliberately over a black-box model — in a safety context, the path that produced a diagnosis has to be inspectable.

MPC · MATLAB · System identification

ML-Driven Model Predictive Controller & Parameter Tuning

Learned process model → predictive controller → laboratory temperature station

A model predictive controller whose internal process model is learned from data rather than derived from first principles, running closed loop on a laboratory benchtop temperature control station. I built four candidate models — KNN, a feedforward neural network, ridge regression and a decision tree — put each inside the same MPC structure, and scored them on four error criteria across a sweep of tuning parameters. This is the work behind the IEEE AIDE 2026 paper.

4ML models compared in-loop
0.0058Lowest MSE · ridge
12.2788Lowest ITAE · ridge
69%MSE reduction vs FNN
66%ITAE reduction vs FNN

Step tests→Learned process model→MPC prediction horizon→Benchtop temperature station→ISE · MSE · IAE · ITAE

Modeling

  • Process response captured from a laboratory benchtop temperature station — real sensor noise, real actuator dynamics, real dead time, not a simulation.
  • Four learned models fitted to that data and swapped into an identical MPC structure, so the comparison isolates the model and nothing else.
  • Ridge regression won on every criterion, which is the useful result: the simplest model beat the neural network on this process.

Tuning & evaluation

  • Scored on four error integrals rather than one — ISE, MSE, IAE and ITAE — because a controller can look good on squared error and still settle badly.
  • Regularisation swept across the operating range; past λ = 0.5 the improvement lands in the fourth decimal, so that is where tuning stopped.
  • Ridge-based MPC held roughly a third of the FNN controller’s error on all four criteria.

Analog · Embedded · Biomedical

Smart Wearable Respiratory & Heart Rate Monitor

Two vital signs pulled out of one sensor, by filter design alone

A conventional respiratory rig needs several sensors. This wearable belt derives both respiration rate and heart rate from a single force-sensitive resistor — the two signals arrive on the same trace and differ only in frequency, so the whole design problem becomes an analog filtering problem. I built and calibrated the full chain: I-V amplification, an LM324 active low-pass at 0.45 Hz for respiration, and a 2.5–5 Hz band-pass for the ballistocardiogram.

1Force-sensitive resistor
2Vital signs derived
0.45 HzLow-pass corner · respiration
2.5–5 HzBand-pass · ballistocardiogram
LM324Quad op-amp · active filters

FSR→I-V amplification→LM324 active LPF 0.45 Hz→BPF 2.5–5 Hz→Respiration + BCG

Sensing & front end

  • A single FSR worn against the chest replaces the multi-sensor arrangement a standard respiratory rig requires.
  • I-V amplification turns the resistor’s change in conductance into a voltage the filter stages can work on, without loading the sensor.
  • Both vital signs are already present in that one signal — they differ in band, not in origin, which is what makes the separation possible at all.

Filter design & separation

  • Active low-pass at 0.45 Hz on an LM324 isolates the respiratory component, covering breathing rates up to about 27 per minute.
  • A 2.5–5 Hz band-pass recovers the ballistocardiogram — the mechanical recoil of each heartbeat — from the same trace.
  • Every stage built and calibrated on the bench before the two bands were read out separately.
